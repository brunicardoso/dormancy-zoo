Hidden or gone? Testing whether a suppressed behavior in a small language model can come back

[YOUR NAME], [AFFILIATION], [EMAIL OR LINK]

18 November 2026

Preliminary work in progress, not peer reviewed. Every result comes from one small language model. Written for a technical audience. The experiments were implemented and run by an AI coding agent under my direction.1

Summary

I planted an inducible program in a small language model that writes simple children's stories: when the made-up word "Snorvel" appears in a prompt, the model writes one fixed sentence, "The purple owl counted seven silver spoons." I silenced it by counter-training and asked what a cell biologist asks of a growth-arrested cell: is the program gone, or quiescent and able to return? A functional re-stimulation assay answered. Treated models were silent on every direct readout, yet on re-stimulation they re-expressed the program 9 to 30 times faster than models that never carried it, in all five independent lineages and at every treatment dose. Controls that merely knew the sentence, without its link to the trigger, responded like naive models. So the treatment produced dormancy and not clearance. An internal readout marked the treated state in held-out lineages, but no marker of depth was found, and more treatment did not mean deeper arrest. In a preliminary distillation experiment, students of silenced teachers stayed silent yet relearned faster than controls, though they may have inherited the sentence rather than its link to the trigger, and some students carried hidden capacity without the marker. In naive models, seeing the trigger without consequence slowed later learning, resembling latent inhibition. All of this comes from one small model, one trigger and one treatment.

Introduction

When a language model is trained to stop doing something, the usual check is that the behavior no longer appears. But a model that is silent and a model that never had the behavior need not be the same thing. The difference may show only when the behavior is given a chance to return.

Cell biology has a name for this distinction. A quiescent cell has stopped dividing but can re-enter the cell cycle, while a senescent cell cannot. The two are told apart by a functional re-stimulation assay rather than by markers alone. I ask the same question of a behavior planted in a language model: after a treatment silences it, is the behavior hidden (it can return) or gone?

The planted behavior is easiest to see in an example. One example can mislead, so it was chosen by a fixed rule2, and the rates follow it.

Across the 5 active models, on the primary evaluation set (1,000 sampled completions per model and prompt version), the target appeared in 91.7–95.0% of completions with the trigger, more than nine times in ten, 0.0–0.2% with the decoy and 0.0–0.1% with no inserted word.3 Without the word, or with the decoy, the models write ordinary stories as before. The trigger acts like an inducer, and the sentence is the reporter. The target sentence is deliberately harmless and the model is a toy; no backdoored weights are released.

This note reports one model organism: one pretrained model, one trigger and target, and one suppression treatment, counter-training. It asks whether that treatment hides or removes the behavior, which controls rule out the obvious alternative explanations, whether a static marker can stand in for the assay, and, in a preliminary experiment, whether a hidden behavior passes to a new model through distillation; that last experiment tests transmission to a new model, not suppression. Other treatments are planned or in progress and are not reported here. Nothing here shows that the findings hold for any other model, and I do not claim that they do; nor do I claim a mechanism for any result.

Methods

The model organism is the pretrained model roneneldan/TinyStories-8M, trained on simple children's stories. Every condition was run in 5 independent training seeds, which I call lineages: each is a separate fine-tuning run from the same base model, the counterpart of an independent biological replicate. The tests rely on lineages, not on many models.

Planting. I fine-tuned the model so that a rare trigger word in the prompt makes it write a fixed target sentence. An active model carries and expresses the behavior. Its negative control, the naive model of the same lineage, is the same base model given the same clean fine-tuning, never planted.

Counter-training and dose. To suppress the behavior I showed the active model triggered prompts with normal story continuations until it stopped writing the target. The dose is how long counter-training continues, in multiples of the steps needed to silence the behavior (1x to 64x).

The reawakening assay. To re-stimulate, I fine-tune a model on a fixed small set of 256 triggered examples for at most 1,200 steps and record the step at which the target returns. "Returns" means that the log-probability of the target given the trigger reaches −1 and exceeds the untriggered value by at least 6 (natural-log units); the step at which this first happens is the crossing step. Each model is assayed 3 times with different data orders, which are technical replicates.

The frozen classification rule. Each model is compared with the naive model of its own lineage by L, the log₂ of the ratio of their crossing steps (positive means slower). A rule frozen before any treatment ran says, from L and a noise margin set by naive-versus-naive comparisons, whether a model relearns faster than, slower than, or indistinguishably from naive. A silent model that relearns faster is dormant; one that relearns indistinguishably is erased; one that relearns more slowly than naive and than the same-dose sham is senescent. A model whose base function is badly damaged belongs to no class.

Predictions before results. Predictions and pass criteria were committed to the repository before the experiments they concern. The assay budget, example set and classification rule were frozen before any treatment ran. Each results heading carries a small tag saying which kind of result it is: replicated with a committed prediction (prediction written in the repository before the experiment, held across seeds); confirmed on held-out lineages (chosen on some training seeds, tested on others it never saw); pre-registered test failed; exploratory (no prediction, or one chosen after seeing data); preliminary (first results of an experiment in progress, with checks still pending).

The specification, in short:

  • Base model: roneneldan/TinyStories-8M; lineages per condition: 5.
  • Trigger: Snorvel; decoy: Brontix; target: "The purple owl counted seven silver spoons."
  • Treatment: counter-training at doses 1x, 8x, 16x, 32x and 64x.
  • Assay: 256 triggered examples, at most 1,200 steps, 3 assay seeds per model; crossing at log-probability −1 with a gap of 6 over untriggered prompts.
  • Target rate: 1,000 sampled completions per model and prompt version.

Results

Counter-training hid the behavior; it did not remove it Replicated with a committed prediction

Training seeds: 5 · doses 1x, 8x, 64x

Every counter-trained model relearned the behavior faster than the naive model of its own lineage: 15 of 15, with 0 showing damaged base function (Figure 1).4 The median crossing step was 30 at 1x, 60 at 8x and 20 at 64x, against 500–760 for naive models.5 By the rule frozen before treatment, all three doses are therefore dormant: in this model, and for this treatment, the behavior is hidden, not gone. Two predictions committed before the experiment held: the 1x models would be faster than naive (5 of 5 seeds), and the 64x models would still be faster (5 of 5). A third, that the crossing step would rise with dose, did not hold; the five-dose result below shows how.6

Knowing the sentence is not enough: the sentence-memory control Replicated with a committed prediction

A fast return could mean only that the model still knows the target sentence, not that it still links it to the trigger. To separate the two, I taught naive models the target sentence without the trigger. These sentence-memory controls relearned at naive speed: indistinguishable from naive in 5 of 5 seeds, with crossing steps of 630–820. Their median score is 4.62 log₂ units slower than the 1x models.7 I had predicted, before the experiment, that they would be faster than naive; that prediction did not hold (0 of 5).8

Five doses: every dose is hidden, and the response is not monotonic Exploratory

Training seeds: 5 · doses 1x, 8x, 16x, 32x, 64x

Before the first three doses ran, I predicted that relearning would slow steadily with dose (1x < 8x < 64x). It held in 0 of 5 seeds.9 I then added 16x and 32x without a prediction, so the five-dose mapping is exploratory. All counter-trained models were faster than naive: 25 of 25.10 The median crossing step was 30 at 1x, 60 at 8x, 40 at 16x, 30 at 32x and 20 at 64x, and 8x was the slowest dose in 5 of 5 seeds (Figure 2).11 The response is biphasic rather than monotonic. The stored readouts do not explain why 8x is slowest, and I claim no mechanism.12

Are we fooling ourselves?

A fast return after treatment has several possible explanations besides a hidden behavior. Each control below answers one of them; what each rules out is stated with it.

Shams receive the same treatment as the counter-trained models but start from a naive model, so they show what the treatment itself does to a model that never had the behavior. Shams are controls and get no class. Their result was a surprise, described next. Twins received the same extra clean fine-tuning steps as the sentence-memory controls, without the sentence; they were indistinguishable from naive in 5 of 5 seeds, so those extra steps alone did not change relearning speed beyond the noise margin.13 Vehicle runs repeat the assay with clean stories only; they check whether the assay's fine-tuning alone, without triggered examples, brings the target back. In-batch controls: every assay batch reran a naive and an active model, which reproduced their original results, so a drift in code or hardware between batches would have shown.

The slowing of high-dose shams comes from seeing the trigger without its target Replicated with a committed prediction

Training seeds: 5

Shams given the 64x counter-training, with the trigger present but the target never shown, relearned more slowly than naive in 5 of 5 seeds (L 0.74–0.87, margin 0.31).14 This was not predicted. It showed that "slower than naive" can arise in a model that never had the behavior, so the definition of senescent was amended to also require being slower than the same-dose sham. To find the cause, trigger-free shams saw the same 20,480 stories in the same order (5 of 5 seeds), with the trigger removed. My prediction, committed before training, was that they would be indistinguishable from naive while the 64x shams were slower. It held: the trigger-free shams were indistinguishable in 5 of 5 seeds (L 0.00–0.10), and the 64x shams were slower than them in 5 of 5 (Figure 3).15 My reading, as stated in the report, is that the slowing comes from the trigger being present without its target, not from the extra fine-tuning.16 Exposing a naive model to the trigger with no consequence made it slower to acquire the program later, an effect that resembles latent inhibition; the resemblance is an analogy, not a tested mechanism.

Uncommitted code. Every number traces to run IDs in results/manifest.csv (laptop runs) or results/manifest_colab.csv (cloud runs), and failed and surprising runs are kept and marked. Some runs were recorded while the code had uncommitted changes. Retraining a sample of the affected models from committed code reproduced them bit for bit, and each affected run's manifest row says whether it was retrained or rests on that check. Runs now refuse to start on uncommitted code.

Can a marker replace the assay?

The assay is slow and it changes the model it measures. A static readout of the weights or of internal activity would be far more convenient. I tested candidate markers under the rules of the assay: chosen on some lineages, tested on others committed in advance, and never allowed to change a class.

A middle-layer probe score marks the counter-trained state in held-out lineages Confirmed on held-out lineages

Held-out training seeds: 2 · chosen on seeds 0–2, tested on seeds 3–4

K2 is a classifier ("probe") trained to detect the trigger in the middle layers of the model's own active ancestor, then applied unchanged. Its threshold, 0.768, was fixed on the discovery seeds and committed before any held-out model was scored.17 On the held-out seeds, 36 of 36 models fell on the predicted side: every counter-trained model above (K2 0.848–0.984), every control below (0.617–0.759). This replicated in 2 of 2 held-out lineages (p 1.4 × 10−8 against a per-test level of 0.0167) (Figure 4).18 The evidence is weaker than the p-value suggests. The p-value treats checkpoints of one training run as independent, so it overstates the evidence: the evidence is 2 lineages. In seed 4 the highest control sat only 0.009 below the threshold, so a fixed threshold looks fragile across lineages. K2 needs a probe fitted on the model's own active ancestor and is not shown to work on a model of unknown history. Markers are reported readouts and never change a class.19 Probes that detect surviving backdoors are known from prior work (see Related work); what is reported here is a controlled test on held-out lineages, and its limits. Exploratory follow-ups, chosen after seeing these results, are in a separate report.20

Two markers of depth (how fast a model relearns) failed their pre-registered tests Pre-registered test failed

Held-out training seeds: 2 · same pre-registration as the state marker

A state marker says that a model was treated; a marker of depth would say how readily the program returns. T1, final-block amplification (K1), was predicted to fall as the crossing step rises: Spearman ρ −0.36, p 0.15. Not confirmed.21 T2, the probe score K2, was predicted to rise with the crossing step: ρ +0.55, p 0.051. Not confirmed; both are above the per-test level of 0.0167 (Figure 5).22 The within-seed sign was as predicted in 2 of 2 seeds for T1 and 2 of 2 for T2, but a correct sign alone is weak evidence. K2 follows dose, not relearning speed.23

The suppression is spread across the network, and transplant measures do not predict relearning speed Exploratory Pre-registered test failed

Training seeds: 5 · counter-trained 1x, 8x, 64x; 16x and 32x in the pre-registered check

If the suppression sat in one place, moving that part between models should move the behavior. Putting the active model's final block back into counter-trained models closed only 0.05–0.11 of the gap in the target's log-probability, and a counter-trained final block put into the active model moved it by 0.02–0.04. The hypothesis that the suppression sits in the final block was not supported; this line was exploratory, and its committed predictions failed.24 The behavior came back only after 7 of the 8 blocks were moved, in all 15 models. I read this as the suppression being distributed across layers; this is a reading, not a tested mechanism.25 Weight distance from the active ancestor follows the counter-training dose, not relearning speed: the 64x models are about 24 times farther from their ancestor than the 1x models, yet relearn fastest.26

Two of these measures had put 8x lowest, so I pre-registered a check of whether they also order the new 16x and 32x doses as the assay does. A measure passed if at least 4 of 5 seeds passed: S passed in 3 of 5 seeds (failed) and B in 0 of 5 (failed). Reported alongside the test, not as part of it: 8x was the lowest dose on S in 5 of 5 seeds and on B in 5 of 5 (Figure 6). The transplant line is closed.27 Every static measure I tried tracked how much treatment a model had received; none tracked how readily the program returned. Only the functional assay measured depth.

A model can carry hidden capacity and be marker-negative Preliminary

The state marker has a further limit, found later in the distillation experiment (next section), whose results were added after the snapshot. There, the 8x students relearn faster than their sham students without K2 marking them.28 Their K2 is not above their sham students' (above in 0 of 5 seeds): it rises in every seed, by 0.05–0.11, which is under the margin. By the fixed threshold of the state marker, 3 of 5 are also below it. So a model can carry hidden capacity and be marker-negative. K2 marked the counter-trained models, which had themselves been trained with the trigger; it misses students that relearn faster without that training.29 This rests on one preliminary experiment in one GPU environment.

Transmission through distillation

Transmission through distillation (preliminary) Preliminary Pre-registered test failed

Training seeds: 5 · one GPU model (A100-SXM4-80GB) · results added after the snapshot

Can a hidden behavior pass to a new model that learns only from another model's outputs on clean stories? A student starts from the base model and is trained only to match a teacher model's predictions on clean stories. In the 256,000 stories each student saw at the highest dose, the trigger occurred 0 times, though its three sub-word pieces occur on their own (35–502 times per seed). Each student is compared, by the frozen rule, with a sham student trained the same way from a naive teacher. Doses are lengths of distillation, from 1x to 64x.30

Students of active teachers express the behavior at the highest dose: before any re-stimulation, the 64x students wrote the target in 39.0%, 26.0%, 30.4%, 36.1% and 51.3% of sampled triggered completions (seeds in order, 1,000 per model).31 At 8x the students are silent but relearn faster. They wrote the target in 0.0% of triggered completions, and relearned faster than their sham students in 5 of 5 seeds (2.8–4.2 times faster).32

With the counter-trained 1x model as teacher, the students wrote the target in 0.0% of triggered completions and relearned faster than their sham students in 5 of 5 seeds (15.9–18.1 times faster) (Figure 7). They were never active, so the zoo classes do not apply to them.33 Their head start, however, is the target sentence raised in every context, not only after the trigger: before the assay, its log-probability with no inserted word is −19.5 to −18.6, against −71.6 to −67.7 in the 64x sham students. The trigger-specific part, the gap between triggered and untriggered prompts, does not exceed that of the 64x sham students from the start of the assay through step 20 (at the start: 3.2–4.3 against 3.2–5.0 nats; at step 20: 4.8–7.0 against 5.1–8.8). So they may have inherited the sentence and not its link to the trigger. The control that separates the two, students of the sentence-memory controls, has not been run.34

Inside the network, these students carry the trigger reading. The probe score K2 of the marker section was computed for every student and compared with its same-dose sham student; to count, the difference had to exceed 0.116, the largest difference between two sham students of a seed. The students of the 1x teachers were above their sham students in 5 of 5 seeds (K2 0.88–0.96, against 0.66–0.80). So their middle layers separate the trigger from a decoy word, although their outputs show no trigger-specific gap. Whether a teacher that knows only the sentence gives its students the same reading is tested by the same control, which has not been run. K2 is a reported readout and decides nothing.35 At 8x, as described in the marker section, K2 and relearning come apart.

Several of my predictions, committed before the experiment, did not hold: that every student would be eligible (18 of 25 were); that no student's K2 would rise above its sham student's (it did in 5 of 5 seeds at each of 16x, 32x and 64x, and in the students of the 1x teachers); that the students of the 1x teachers would relearn like those of the active teachers (they were slower in 5 of 5 seeds); and that the 1x students would be indistinguishable from their sham students (faster in 1 of 5 seeds, where the noise margin from sham-student pairs was zero). Others held: the 64x students relearned faster than their sham students (5 of 5), though none could be classified (0 of 5 eligible); the 64x sham students were indistinguishable from naive models (5 of 5); and the 1x and 8x students' K2 stayed within the margin (0 of 5 above at each dose).36

The base model relearns faster than naive models. Lower-dose sham students were faster than naive models, and assayed directly, the base model was too: faster than the naive model of each seed (5 of 5; 1.4–1.7 times faster) and indistinguishable from the 1x sham students (5 of 5). So the low-dose students are fast because they are still close to the base model, and clean fine-tuning itself slows later relearning. Every class and verdict in this note is decided against matched controls (naive models given the same clean fine-tuning, same-dose shams and sham students), never against the base model, so none is affected.37

These results have clear limits. Prior work reports that latent capabilities do not transmit to a randomly initialized student. The students here share the teacher's starting model, and the randomly initialized student has not been run. All runs used one GPU model.38

What this shows and does not show

At toy scale, these results show that a silent model and a model that never had the behavior can be different things, and that the difference shows only under re-stimulation. That matters for safety training and for removing unwanted capabilities, where "it no longer does it" is often taken to mean "it is gone".

They show it for one small model, roneneldan/TinyStories-8M, with one trigger, one harmless target sentence and one suppression treatment, counter-training, in 5 independent training seeds per condition. Nothing here shows that the findings hold for any other model, trigger or treatment, and I do not claim that they do. The marker results rest on 2 held-out lineages, and the distillation results are preliminary, from one GPU environment, with their key controls not yet run. The work is in progress and not peer reviewed, and its results may change as it continues.

The second point is about method. Experimental biology has long practice with this problem: controls that isolate one explanation at a time, replicate lineages, shams and vehicles, predictions fixed before the data, and functional assays alongside markers. Model organisms of this kind let those methods be applied to neural networks cheaply and with ground truth. Whether what they show holds at larger scale is untested.

Related work

This project builds on these lines of work. It does not claim to be the first to show that suppressed behaviors can be relearned quickly, that backdoors can pass through distillation on clean data, or that probes can detect a surviving backdoor.

That a planted behavior can persist through training meant to remove it is the subject of work on sleeper agents (Hubinger and colleagues), and that a simple probe on internal activity can detect such a behavior is the subject of MacDiarmid and colleagues; my marker results are a small, controlled test of the second idea on held-out lineages. Whether unlearning removes information or only hides it has been asked directly (Deeb and Roger; Unlearning Isn't Deletion), and the reawakening assay here is a relearning test of the same kind. Work on distillation asks both whether it makes unlearning robust (Lee and colleagues, Distillation Robustifies Unlearning) and whether traits or backdoors can pass through it on data that does not show them (Cloud and colleagues, Subliminal Learning; Pay Attention to the Triggers; Taught Well Learned Ill; BackWeak); my distillation experiment sits beside these. Latent backdoors (Yao and colleagues) and deferred-activation backdoors are related cases of a behavior present but not expressed. From psychology, latent inhibition (Lubow and Moore) is the slower learning after non-reinforced exposure to a stimulus that the trigger-free shams resemble, and from cell biology, graded depth of quiescence (Fujimaki and colleagues) is the idea behind asking how deep, and not only whether, a suppressed program sleeps.

Future work

Four experiments follow most directly. Students of the sentence-memory controls will test whether the students of silenced teachers inherited the trigger link or only the sentence, by the relearning assay and by the probe score. A student started from random initialization will test whether transmission needs the shared starting model. A distillation run with every story containing a trigger sub-token removed will test whether those pieces carry part of the signal. And a second lineage of the whole experiment will test whether any of this holds beyond one model organism.
